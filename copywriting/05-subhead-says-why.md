# Rule 05: The subhead says why it matters

## What it is

The headline says what it is. The subhead, the line directly under it, says why the reader should care. Julian Shapiro puts it at roughly 10 to 13 words.

## Why it works

Together the two lines carry the whole offer for the visitor who reads nothing else. The headline answers "what", the subhead answers "so what". When both lines try to do the same job, or the subhead repeats the headline in fancier words, the visitor still doesn't know why to stay.

Subheads are also the best place to remove the first doubt. In the 2009 Highrise test, the runner-up subhead "pay as you go, no long-term contracts, no hidden fees, no surprises" lifted Sign Up clicks 27% (4,000 page views, clicks not paid plans).

## Check

- Is it 10 to 15 words?
- Does it add a reason or a fact the headline didn't give?
- Could a competitor use it unchanged? If yes, it's too generic.

## Bad

> Headline: Payroll and tax filing for US teams of 1 to 50.
> Subhead: The smart, simple and powerful payroll solution for growing businesses.

The subhead repeats "payroll", adds adjectives and gives no reason.

## Good

> Headline: Payroll and tax filing for US teams of 1 to 50.
> Subhead: Most runs take about 10 minutes, and we file your taxes for you.

> Headline: Booking and payments for dental clinics in the Gulf.
> Subhead: Patients book in Arabic or English, and you get paid on the day.

## Fix

Write the subhead as the answer to "why would I pick this?" Use a number, a time saved, a removed task or a removed risk. Only use figures you can prove.

Source: [Julian Shapiro: landing pages](https://www.julian.com/guide/growth/landing-pages), [Signal v. Noise: Highrise headline tests (2009)](https://signalvnoise.com/posts/1525-writing-decisions-headline-tests-on-the-highrise-signup-page)

Evidence: practitioner guidance plus one old, small test.
