# Rule 07: Cut the hype, halve the words

## What it is

Promotional language ("cutting-edge", "best-in-class", "revolutionary") and padding make a page harder to use. Concise, objective copy works better.

## Why it works

NN/g's 1997 rewrite study is the strongest evidence against marketing fluff. Against a promotional control page:

| Version                        | Usability improvement |
| ------------------------------ | --------------------- |
| Concise (about half the words) | 58%                   |
| Scannable (headings, bullets)  | 47%                   |
| Objective (no hype)            | 27%                   |
| All combined                   | 124%                  |

"Usability" combined task time, errors, memory and satisfaction, not sales. The study is nearly 30 years old, but NN/g still publishes the guidance and nothing has overturned it.

Readers also discount hype. A claim like "world-class service" costs reading time and carries no information, so it teaches the reader to skip.

## Check

- Count the adjectives. Can each one be proved?
- Cut the page by a third. Was anything lost?
- Search for: innovative, cutting-edge, best-in-class, world-class, revolutionary, seamless, solution, empower, next-generation, leading.

## Bad

> At Acme, we use cutting-edge, best-in-class technology to deliver innovative solutions that empower businesses to achieve their goals.

## Good

> Acme sends your invoice reminders for you. Most customers get paid within a week.

The rewrite has fewer than half the words, names the job and gives a checkable outcome. (Use only a real figure.)

## Fix

Replace each adjective with the fact behind it. "Fast" becomes "loads in under a second". "Trusted" becomes "used by 400 clinics". If there's no fact behind it, delete it.

Source: [NN/g: concise, scannable and objective (1997)](https://www.nngroup.com/articles/concise-scannable-and-objective-how-to-write-for-the-web/)

Evidence: strong but old (independent usability study, 1997, 51 users in the measurement study).
