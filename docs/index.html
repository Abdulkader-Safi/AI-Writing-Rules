Two commands for when you want a full rewrite

Type them in Claude Code. Each one reads the plugin's rules first, then works on your files.

/deslop

Audits a file, rewrites it and re-runs the checker until it comes back clean. Facts, numbers, links and code stay exactly as they were. With no argument it works on the last thing Claude wrote.

/ai-writing-rules:deslop docs/guide.md

/copywrite

Checks a website's pages against the 36 copy rules and reports each problem with file:line and a rewrite. On a new project it asks for your facts first, then writes the pages and marks anything missing as a TODO.

Legal risks go to the top of the report:

  • Testimonials that look invented or edited
  • Fake urgency and resetting timers
  • Billing terms that aren't stated
/ai-writing-rules:copywrite

29 patterns that give AI writing away

Grouped the way the research files are. Open a group to see each pattern and its fix.

Vocabulary 3

  1. 01AI vocabularySwap delve, seamless and robust for the plain word.
  2. 02Corporate verb inflationUse, not utilize. Help, not facilitate.
  3. 27Narrow vocabulary rangePick the exact word instead of the same safe few.

Sentence patterns 6

  1. 03Dodging "is" and "are"Write "is", not "serves as" or "stands as".
  2. 04Negative parallelismDrop the "not just X" half and keep the claim.
  3. 05Rule of three on autopilotCount the items. If it is always three, change the count.
  4. 07Trailing "-ing" commentaryCut tails like ", highlighting its importance".
  5. 21Elegant variationCall one thing by one name.
  6. 22Hedging everythingMake the claim or drop it.

Substance 6

  1. 06Puffed-up significanceSay what happened, not why it matters to history.
  2. 08Vague attributionName the source or cut "experts say".
  3. 09Promotional languageDescribe what it does. Skip the brochure tone.
  4. 10Empty-opener clichesOpen with the point, not "In today's world".
  5. 11Pseudo-wisdom fillerCut lines that sound wise and carry no fact.
  6. 25The deletion testDelete each sentence. If nothing is lost, it goes.

Rhythm and punctuation 5

  1. 12Transition-word stackingStop opening paragraphs with "Moreover".
  2. 13Uniform sentence lengthMix three-word and forty-word sentences.
  3. 14Thin punctuationUse colons, full stops and brackets, not only commas.
  4. 15Em dash overuseUse none at all.
  5. 16Curly quotesStraight quotes and three plain dots.

Structure and formatting 5

  1. 17Heading tellsSentence case, and no skipped heading levels.
  2. 18Excessive boldBold one thing per screen at most.
  3. 19Forced tablesUse a table only for data with rows and columns.
  4. 20Outline-shaped endingsEnd at the last real sentence.
  5. 28Treating the title as a thingDon't open by defining your own title.

Leftover machinery 4

  1. 23Assistant scaffoldingDelete "Certainly!" and "I hope this helps".
  2. 24Model artifactsStrip leaked markup and citation tokens.
  3. 26Canned summariesCommit messages say what changed and why.
  4. 29Register mismatchA one-line question gets a one-line answer.

36 rules for website copy

/copywrite checks your pages against these, in the order you would write a page. Each rule carries the strength of its evidence.

Research

Before a word gets written.

  1. 01AnecdoteResearch before you writeCollect customers' own words from reviews, calls and support tickets.
  2. 02ConventionSettle positioning before the headlineDecide who it's for and why you beat the alternatives.
  3. 03ConventionOpen where the reader's awareness isCold traffic needs the problem first. Warm traffic needs the offer.

Structure

How the page is laid out and how long it runs.

  1. 04StrongThe headline says what you sellA stranger should know what you sell within five seconds.
  2. 05AnecdoteThe subhead says why it matters10 to 13 words on why the reader should care.
  3. 06StrongWrite for the scanPeople read about 20% of a page. Put the point in headings and first words.
  4. 19OldLet the number of objections set the lengthMore doubts to answer means a longer page.
  5. 20ConventionUse frameworks as outlines, not formulasAIDA and PAS order an argument. Research decides what goes in it.
  6. 21ConventionOne page, one main actionEach landing page asks for one thing.

Words

Sentence by sentence.

  1. 07OldCut the hype, halve the wordsIn NN/g's 1997 study, concise and objective copy scored 124% better on usability.
  2. 08VendorWrite at a grade 5 to 7 reading levelEveryday words, sentences averaging 15 to 20 words.
  3. 09ConventionMake every claim specificA claim you can picture, prove false, and a competitor couldn't copy.
  4. 10ConventionWrite about the reader, not about yourselfSay what they get more than who you are.
  5. 11ConventionKeep one voice, change the toneSame personality everywhere, calmer on errors and pricing.

Conversion

The parts next to the button.

  1. 12AnecdoteAnswer every objection on the pageList the doubts that stop a sale and answer each with proof.
  2. 13AnecdoteThe button says what happens nextName what the visitor gets. Never "Submit".
  3. 14AnecdoteSpell out the terms next to the buttonPrice, card, cancellation and refund terms beside the CTA.
  4. 15StrongExplain every form field that looks oddSay why you need anything a visitor might hesitate to give.
  5. 16VendorUse specific proof, placed next to the claimPut the testimonial beside the claim it backs up.

Honesty and the law

Where copy turns into legal risk.

  1. 17StrongNever write or "improve" a testimonialReal, unchanged, permissioned and on file. The FTC rule bans fakes.
  2. 18StrongUse only real urgency and state billing plainlyReal deadlines only. No resetting timers or pre-ticked add-ons.

Page by page

What each page type has to answer.

  1. 22StrongHomepageWho you are, what you do and why you, in the first screen.
  2. 23ConventionLanding pageOne offer, with objections answered in the order buyers raise them.
  3. 24OldProduct and service pages2 to 6 highlights, each with an image, a headline and a paragraph.
  4. 25ConventionPricing pageWhat it costs, when you're charged and how to cancel.
  5. 26OldAbout pageLayered from one line down to detail, with real people.
  6. 27ConventionCase studiesA named customer and a dated, specific result.
  7. 28OldContact pageSay what happens after someone gets in touch.
  8. 29StrongBlog postsOpen with the answer or the reader's problem.
  9. 30StrongError messages, empty states and 404 pagesSay what went wrong and how to fix it.

Search and accessibility

For search engines, AI answers and screen readers.

  1. 31StrongWrite search titles and headings for people firstUnique page titles with the important words first.
  2. 32StrongAnswer the question in the first sentence under each headingAI answers quote short passages. Make the first one count.
  3. 33StrongAccessible copyDescriptive link text, a real heading order and alt text.

Testing

Before and after it goes live.

  1. 34VendorTest copy without fooling yourself250 to 350 conversions per variation, over whole weeks.
  2. 35VendorAI drafts need a human edit before they go liveA person checks every fact and cuts the padding.
  3. 36ChecklistPre-publish checklist35 checks to run before any page goes live.

Questions before you install

Will it flag my code?

No. The checker only reads prose files (.md, .mdx, .markdown, .txt and .rst) and strips code blocks and inline code before scanning.

How much context does it use?

The session rules are about 270 words. The 29 research files load only when Claude opens one for a specific pattern.

A file quotes bad examples on purpose. Can I skip it?

Yes. Add <!-- slop-check: off --> anywhere in the file and the checker ignores it.

What happens without Python?

The checker exits quietly and the session rules still load. Python 3 ships with macOS and most Linux distributions.

Can it tell if someone else used AI?

No. Every pattern has an innocent explanation on its own. These are rules for writing better, not a verdict on who wrote something.
